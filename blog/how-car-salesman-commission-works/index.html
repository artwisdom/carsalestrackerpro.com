How Car Salesman Commission Really Works

If your pay plan reads like a foreign language, you're not alone. Here's exactly how a car salesperson's commission is built — gross, pack, minis, back-end, draws and bonuses — with real numbers you can sanity-check against your own check.

Published by Michael DubeCreator of CarSales Tracker Pro · methodology & corrections
The short version

You're paid a percentage (usually 20–30%) of each car's gross profit, often after a "pack" is subtracted, with a flat "mini" on low-profit deals. Add back-end and bonuses, subtract your draw, and that's your check. Run your own numbers →

1. It starts with gross profit

Nearly every dealership pays salespeople on gross profit — not the price of the car. Gross is the difference between what the customer pays and what the store has in the vehicle. Sell a used car for $24,000 that the dealership has $21,000 in, and the front-end gross is $3,000.

Your commission is a slice of that gross. The most common front-end rate is 20% to 30%. On that $3,000 deal at 25%, you'd earn $750 — before the wrinkles below.

2. Pack: the cut taken before you're paid

Most stores subtract a fixed pack from the gross before calculating your commission. Pack covers things like reconditioning and overhead, and it usually runs $300–$800 per car (sometimes more on used).

Back to our $3,000 deal: with an $800 pack, you're paid on $2,200, not $3,000. At 25%, that's $550 instead of $750. Pack is the single biggest reason a deal "felt" bigger than your check — always know your store's pack.

3. Mini deals: the floor on thin deals

Plenty of cars sell at little or no front gross — a price-shopper, an aged unit, a tough trade. A percentage of almost nothing is almost nothing, so dealerships pay a flat mini instead, typically $50–$150.

The rule is simple: if the percentage would pay you less than the mini, you get the mini. Sell ten cars in a slow month and five of them are minis, and you start to understand why volume — and protecting your gross — matters so much. We break this down further in What Is a Mini Deal?

4. Back-end (F&I) gross

When the finance manager sells a warranty, GAP, or a better rate, that profit is back-end gross. Some pay plans cut you in — often a smaller percentage than front-end — and some don't. If yours does, your effective pay per car can be meaningfully higher than the front-end number alone. Check whether your plan pays on back-end, and at what rate.

5. Volume bonuses and spiffs

On top of per-car commission, most plans layer extras:

  • Volume bonus: hit a unit count (say 12, 15, 18 cars) and earn a bonus — sometimes a flat sum, sometimes a per-car kicker applied to the whole month. This is why the 12th car can be worth far more than the 11th.
  • Spiffs: cash for moving a specific aged unit, a slow model, or a manufacturer push. Easy money if you know what's spiffed.
  • CSI / survey bonuses: some stores tie a bonus to customer-satisfaction scores.

6. The draw (and why your check can feel small)

Many salespeople are paid a draw — a guaranteed weekly or monthly advance against future commission. If you draw $1,500/month and earn $4,000 in commission, you get the $4,000 (the draw was an advance, not extra). Have a slow month and earn less than your draw, and you may owe it back against future deals. Know whether your draw is recoverable or not.

FAQ

What percentage of gross do car salesmen make?
Front-end commission is most commonly 20–30% of gross profit, frequently calculated after a pack is subtracted. Back-end (F&I) percentages, where they exist, are usually lower.
What is pack in car sales?
Pack is a fixed amount (often $300–$800) the dealership subtracts from a car's gross before paying your commission. It reduces your commissionable gross on every deal.
Why was my commission check smaller than I expected?
Usually pack, minis on thin deals, or a recoverable draw. Track each deal's real gross and commission so payday is never a surprise.
