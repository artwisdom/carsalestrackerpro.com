Front End vs. Back End Gross: Which One Your Commission Is Really Paid On

If you're trying to sort out front end vs back end gross, you're really asking one thing: which pile of money is my commission actually calculated on? Get it wrong and you'll defend the wrong number in the deal, chase the wrong products, and misread your own paycheck. So let's settle it. Every car deal produces profit in two separate places, those two buckets get paid very differently, and on most pay plans one of them is where nearly all your money comes from.

Published by Michael DubeCreator of CarSales Tracker Pro · methodology & corrections
The short version

Front-end gross is the profit on the car itself — selling price minus the store's total cost. Back-end (F&I) gross is the profit on financing, warranties, GAP and the like. Most salespeople are paid a percentage — commonly 20-30% — of front-end gross, and many pay plans pay nothing on the back end. Some pay a smaller slice of it too. Know your split, then run a real deal through the commission calculator to see what it actually pays. And remember: your own store's pay plan is the only thing that governs your check.

What is front-end gross in car sales?

Every retail car deal makes profit in two separate places, and your pay plan treats them as two separate things.

Front-end gross is the profit on the vehicle. It's the selling price minus the dealership's total cost in that car — not invoice, not what you think the car is worth, but the store's actual cost, including reconditioning on a used unit. Sell a car for $28,000 that the store is into for $25,000 and you've got $3,000 of front-end gross before anything else touches it.

What is back-end gross in car sales?

Back-end gross — also called F&I gross, for Finance and Insurance — is the profit made in the finance office after you hand the customer off. That's the markup on the financing (the reserve), plus extended service contracts, GAP, tire-and-wheel, paint and interior protection, and the rest of the menu. The F&I manager builds that gross; you usually don't.

Same customer, same deal, two different profit centers. The question that decides your paycheck is which of these two your commission percentage gets applied to.

Which gross your commission is really paid on: front end vs back end gross

For the large majority of salespeople, commission is paid on front-end gross. That's the number your percentage bites into. The most common rate lands somewhere in the 20-30% range, with 25% a common midpoint — but that percentage is of the front-end gross profit, never the sale price. This trips up new salespeople constantly. A percentage of a $28,000 sticker is a fantasy. A percentage of the $3,000 you actually held on the car is real money.

The back end is where plans diverge. Some pay you a slice of back-end gross — usually a smaller percentage than the front — and many pay you nothing on it at all. On a front-end-only plan, every warranty and GAP contract the finance manager sells is 100% the store's and the finance office's money; you don't see a cent of it directly. Neither approach is "standard." It's a choice each dealer makes, and it's written into your pay plan.

This is exactly why you can't assume. Two salespeople across town from each other can earn very different money on the same deal purely because one store shares back-end gross and the other keeps it. Your own dealership's pay plan is the only thing that governs your pay — not a forum post, not this article, not what your buddy at another brand makes. Read yours, and if a line isn't clear, have your manager walk it with you. Our pay plan breakdown covers what to look for.

Before the percentage: pack comes out first

Here's the part that quietly shrinks a lot of paychecks. On most front-end pay plans, the store pulls a pack out of the gross before your percentage is calculated. Pack is a fixed amount the dealer subtracts off the top — commonly $300 to $800, sometimes higher on used units. It's meant to cover overhead the store assigns to each car, and it comes out whether the deal was fat or thin.

So the gross you get paid on isn't the raw gross. It's raw gross minus pack. On a thin deal that difference is the whole check.

Let's run the earlier deal all the way through. Say you sold that car with $3,000 of front-end gross, the store's pack is $600, and your rate is 25%:

  • Raw front-end gross: $3,000
  • Minus pack: -$600
  • Commissionable gross: $2,400
  • Your 25%: $600

Six hundred dollars — on a deal you might have penciled as a $750 commission (25% of the full $3,000) if you forgot pack was coming out. Same car. If your store also shares a piece of the back end, that number climbs from there. When the gross is thin: the mini floor

Not every deal carries $2,400 of commissionable gross. Plenty carry almost none — a hard-negotiated new car, a heavily discounted unit, an internet price you matched to the dollar. When a percentage of a tiny gross would pay you next to nothing, most plans have a mini: a flat minimum commission, commonly in the $100-$200 range, that you get instead.

The rule is simple: you're paid the percentage OR the mini, whichever is higher. Work a deal down to $400 of commissionable gross and 25% is only $100 — below a $150 mini, so you get the $150. Hold $800 and 25% is $200, above the mini, so you get the $200. Our calculator defaults the mini to $150 so you can see exactly where that floor kicks in for your numbers. Minis are a topic of their own — how to stop living on them is covered in our mini deal guide.

Why the split changes how you sell

Once you know your split, your behavior on the floor should change with it. This isn't theory — it's where the money is.

If you're paid on front-end only (no back-end share): every dollar of front-end gross you hold is a quarter — or whatever your rate is — in your pocket. So you fight for the front. Don't give away $500 of gross to "make it easy"; at 25% that's $125 walking out the door on one concession. Set the price with confidence, justify the value, and stop discounting on reflex the second a customer flinches. The finance office can build all the back-end gross in the world and it does nothing for your check, so protect the number that actually pays you.

If you're paid a slice of back-end too: now a clean turnover to F&I directly helps you. Set the deal up right, mention the value of protection products during your walkaround so the customer isn't blindsided in the box, and hand the finance manager a warm, unrushed buyer instead of a tired one halfway out the door. A well-set-up handoff can add real money to your check when you share in it — and it costs you nothing but a few sentences on the lot.

Either way, know where financing and the monthly payment fit, because customers buy the payment, not the price. Knowing roughly what a longer or shorter term does to a payment keeps you sharp when a buyer starts working backward from a number; a quick pass through an auto loan calculator keeps you honest before you promise something the desk can't hit.

Where the split shows up on paper

All of this stays invisible until you can find it on your commission statement. A good statement shows each deal's front-end gross, the pack that came out, your rate, and — if you share it — a separate line for back-end. If yours only prints a lump sum, that's worth a conversation, because you can't verify a number you can't see, and thin-deal months are exactly when a quiet math error costs you.

FAQ

Is car sales commission paid on front-end or back-end gross?
On most pay plans, your commission is calculated on front-end gross — the profit on the car itself (selling price minus the store's total cost), commonly at a 20-30% rate. Whether you also earn on back-end (F&I) gross depends entirely on your dealership. Some plans pay a smaller percentage of back-end; many pay nothing on it. Your own pay plan is the only thing that decides which applies to you.
What's the difference between front-end and back-end gross?
Front-end gross is the profit made on the vehicle — what you and the desk hold on the price. Back-end (also called F&I) gross is the profit the finance office makes after the handoff: financing markup, extended warranties, GAP, and other protection products. They're two separate profit centers, and pay plans treat them separately.
Do I get paid a percentage of the whole selling price?
No. This is the single most common misunderstanding for new salespeople. Your percentage is applied to front-end gross profit — selling price minus the store's cost — not the sticker or sale price. And on most plans a fixed pack (commonly $300-$800) is subtracted from that gross before your percentage is even calculated, so the commissionable number is smaller still.
Why does my store pay back-end when my friend's store doesn't?
Because sharing back-end gross is a choice each dealer makes, not an industry rule. Some owners share a slice of F&I profit to reward salespeople for clean handoffs; others keep all of it in the finance office. Neither is 'standard.' It's written into each store's pay plan, which is why two salespeople can earn very differently on identical deals.
If I only get paid on front-end, does the finance office's gross help me at all?
Not directly on that deal — you don't earn a percentage of it. But it can help indirectly: strong F&I performance keeps the store profitable, and a healthy back end sometimes funds the volume bonuses or spiffs you can hit. Still, if you're front-end only, your energy on each individual deal belongs on holding front-end gross, because that's what your check is actually built on.
