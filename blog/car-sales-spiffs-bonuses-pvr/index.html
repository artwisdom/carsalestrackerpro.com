Car Sales Spiffs and Bonuses: Chasing the Extras On Top of Commission

Your commission is the floor. It's the number you'd make if you walked in, sold a normal month of cars, and never once thought about which car you sold or when you sold it. Everything on top of that floor — the car sales spiffs and bonuses, the volume tiers, the PVR kickers, the factory money — is where the good months come from. Chase them on purpose and a fifteen-car month can pay like a twenty-car month.

Published by Michael DubeCreator of CarSales Tracker Pro · methodology & corrections
The short version

Base commission is your floor. Spiffs (small immediate cash on specific units), volume bonuses (extra pay for hitting unit tiers), PVR bonuses (tied to your average gross per car), and OEM/factory cash all stack on top of it. In a strong month the bonus layer can rival or beat the commission itself. Learn your store's exact triggers, then aim your inventory and your timing at them. Run any single deal through the commission calculator first so you know what the deal pays before you chase the extras — and remember your own dealership's pay plan is the only thing that actually governs your check.

What actually counts as an 'extra'

This post isn't about how your base commission is calculated. That's the foundation, and we cover it in full in how car salesman commission really works and how pay plans are structured. Read those first if the words gross, pack, and mini don't already mean something to you. Here we treat all of that as the floor and talk only about what stacks on top.

The incentive layer usually breaks into four buckets, and almost every store runs some mix of them:

  • Spiffs — small, immediate cash on a specific unit or product. Move that aged unit off the back row, get $200 today. Sell a paint-and-fabric package, get $50. Spiffs are the desk's way of steering you at a specific car right now.
  • Volume bonuses — extra money for hitting a unit count. Common tiers land at 12, 15, or 20 cars in a month, and the bonus often steps up at each rung.
  • PVR bonuses — pay tied to your average front-end gross per car (Per Vehicle Retail). Hold your average gross across the month and you unlock a per-car kicker.
  • Manufacturer / OEM cash — factory money for selling specific models or hitting brand certifications. This rides on top of whatever the dealership pays you.

None of these are universal. Your store might run all four or just one, and the amounts swing hard by brand, region, and product mix. The only number that governs your pay is the pay plan on your own desk — so the first job is knowing exactly which of these your store actually pays, and at what triggers.

Why car sales spiffs and bonuses can beat the commission itself

Here's the part green peas miss. On a normal deal your commission is a slice of a slice. Front-end commission is most commonly 20-30% of the front-end gross profit — not the sale price — and gross is the selling price minus the dealership's total cost in the car, after a pack of commonly $300-$800 is pulled out first. On a lot of deals that leaves you with a mini: the flat floor of commonly $100-$200 that our calculator defaults to $150. You get the mini whenever the percentage math would pay less than it.

Now run a real month. Say you sell 15 cars and roughly half of them mini out. Your commission side might look like this:

  • 7 average deals at $400 commission each = $2,800
  • 8 minis at $150 each = $1,200
  • Commission subtotal: about $4,000

Not a great month on commission alone. Now stack the extras your store pays on that same 15 units:

  • Volume bonus for hitting the 15-car tier: $1,500
  • PVR bonus of $75/car for holding gross, across all 15: $1,125
  • Three spiffs on aged units at $200 each: $600
  • OEM cash on two certified models at $250 each: $500
  • Bonus subtotal: about $3,725

The extras nearly matched the commission — and every dollar of that $3,725 came from decisions about which cars and how many, not from selling harder on any single deal. That's the whole game. The salesperson who hit 15 units on purpose took home close to $7,700; the one who drifted to 11 and never looked at the tier board pocketed the commission on those units and none of the volume or PVR bonus — a swing of thousands off the same lot, same traffic. To see how that gap compounds over a career, look at how much car salesmen actually make by experience level — most of the spread is the bonus layer, not the base rate.

How to chase spiffs and bonuses on purpose

Chasing the extras isn't about working more ups. It's about aiming the ups you already have. A few things veterans do that rookies don't:

  • Know your tiers cold. Write down where the volume bonus kicks in and what it pays at each rung. If you're at 11 cars on the 28th and the tier hits at 12 for a $1,500 bonus, that twelfth car is worth its commission plus $1,500. That's the deal you discount to close. Don't let a $1,500 bonus die over $500 of gross.
  • Read the spiff board before you walk the lot. When the desk puts $300 on a specific stock number, that's a lit-up car — show it. If two customers would each be happy in a similar unit, steer toward the spiffed one.
  • Protect PVR when the bonus is gross-based. If your kicker pays on average gross per car, a string of minis drags your whole month down, not just those single deals. Sometimes holding an extra $300 on one deal is worth more in PVR bonus across your entire unit count than the commission you'd earn on that one car.
  • Time your minis. If a deal is going to mini out anyway and you're chasing a unit tier, take it — it still counts toward volume. But don't stack low-gross deals early in the month and quietly wreck your PVR average before you've noticed.
  • Ask about OEM money by model. Factory cash often favors specific trims, EVs, or certified pre-owned. If two customers are cross-shopping and one path carries $250 of OEM cash, that's real money you'd otherwise leave on the table for no extra work.

Before you commit to discounting a car to hit a tier, run the base deal through the free commission calculator so you know exactly what it pays on its own. Then add the bonus on top with clear eyes — the trade only makes sense once you can see both numbers. Track it or you'll never see it coming

The number one reason people leave bonus money on the table is they don't know where they stand until the desk tells them on the 30th — too late to do anything about it. If you know you're at 14 units on the 26th and the tier's at 15, you'll move heaven and earth to close one more. If you don't know you're at 14, you coast, and that $1,500 walks off the lot.

Keep your own running count: units sold, your gross per deal, which spiffs you've already earned, and how far you are from the next tier. The floor pays the bills. The extras are what turn a decent month into a great one — but only if you're aiming at them on purpose, and only ever within the triggers your own pay plan actually spells out.

FAQ

What's the difference between a spiff and a bonus in car sales?
A spiff is small, immediate cash on a specific unit or product — move that aged truck today, get $200 now. A bonus is usually tied to a bigger target you hit over the month, like a unit-volume tier (say a payout at 12 or 15 cars) or your average gross per car (PVR). Spiffs steer you toward a car right now; bonuses reward the shape of your whole month. Your store's pay plan is what defines the exact triggers and amounts, so confirm yours in writing.
Can bonuses really pay more than my commission?
In a strong month, yes. Base commission is most commonly 20-30% of front-end gross after pack, and a lot of deals mini out at a flat floor of commonly $100-$200. Stack a volume-tier bonus, a per-car PVR kicker across all your units, a few spiffs, and OEM cash, and that layer can rival or beat your commission subtotal. The key is that the bonus money comes from which cars and how many you sold — decisions you control — not from grinding harder on any one deal.
What is PVR and why do bonuses care about it?
PVR is Per Vehicle Retail — your average front-end gross profit per car. Dealerships track it as a core health metric, and many tie a bonus to it: hold your average gross above some line and you unlock a per-car kicker across your unit count. That's why a run of minis can quietly cost you more than the missed commission on those single deals — it drags down the average that your whole bonus is measured against.
Should I discount a car just to hit a volume tier?
Often, yes — if the math works. If the twelfth car triggers a $1,500 tier bonus, that car is worth its commission plus $1,500, so giving up a few hundred dollars of gross to close it is an easy trade. But run the base deal through the commission calculator first so you know what it pays on its own, then decide with the bonus added on top. Never let a big tier bonus die over a small amount of gross.
How do I know which spiffs and bonuses my store actually pays?
Ask your sales manager for the pay plan in writing and read every line — spiff structure, volume tiers and their payouts, any PVR or gross-based bonus, and how OEM cash flows to you. Pay varies widely by store, brand, and region, so nothing you read online overrides your own plan. Once you know your exact triggers, keep a running count during the month so you always know how close you are to the next dollar.
