Average Car Salesman Commission Per Car (And Why the Average Lies)

Ask ten people what the average car salesman commission per car is and you'll hear one confident number, usually somewhere around three or four hundred bucks. That number isn't a lie exactly. It's just an average, and averages hide the deals that actually make up your month. The real answer is a range, and where you land in it depends on gross, pack, your rate, and how many minis you're dragging behind you. Here's the actual math.

The short version

On a typical pay plan you earn a percentage of a car's front-end gross after pack, commonly 20-30% (call it 25%), with a mini floor (often $100-$200) paid when the percentage would come out lower. So a strong deal might pay $500-$700, an average one $250-$350, and a thin one just the mini. Stack enough minis and your true average per car drops well under what people quote. The one number everyone quotes, and why it's wrong

When somebody tells you the average commission per car is "about $400," they're doing something sneaky without knowing it: they're averaging a handful of fat deals in with a stack of skinny ones and reporting the middle. That middle number describes almost none of your actual deals. You don't get paid the average on any given car. You get paid what that car generated.

Here's the part that trips people up: commission is almost never a percentage of the sale price. It's a percentage of front-end gross profit, the selling price minus what the dealership had in the car. Sell a $35,000 truck the store paid $33,000 for and your commissionable gross is $2,000, not $35,000. Two sales that look identical to the customer can pay wildly differently depending on gross.

So the honest way to answer "what's the average" is to stop hunting for one number and look at the distribution: what a strong deal pays, what an average deal pays, and what a mini pays. Your monthly average is just those three buckets in whatever proportion your month happened to land.

Running a real deal: pack, rate, and the mini floor

Let's use the numbers most stores run and the same defaults the calculator uses: a 25% commission rate and a $150 mini. Before anybody figures your cut, the dealership subtracts a pack, a fixed amount, commonly $300-$800 and sometimes more on used, off the gross. Pack comes out first, then your percentage is calculated on what's left.

Watch what that does across three deals:

  • Strong deal: $3,000 gross minus $500 pack = $2,500, times 25% = $625 to you.
  • Average deal: $1,600 gross minus $500 pack = $1,100, times 25% = $275 to you.
  • Thin deal: $700 gross minus $500 pack = $200, times 25% = $50, which is below the floor, so you're bumped up to the $150 mini.

That third one is the whole story. The percentage math said $50, but the pay plan won't pay you less than the mini, so you keep $150. You get the mini any time the percentage would pay less than it, and on low- or no-gross cars, that's a lot of the time. Plug your own store's pack and rate into the commission calculator and you'll see exactly where your break-even between percentage and mini sits, the gross above which the percentage finally beats the floor.

What a pile of minis does to your average

Now let's build a realistic 12-car month and actually average it, all figures after pack, run at 25% with a $150 floor. Some deals were paydays, most were fine, and a chunk were minis:

  • Two hero cars: one $625 deal and one $425 deal, for $1,050.
  • Four bread-and-butter deals at $275, $225, $200, and $175, for $875.
  • Six deals that hit the $150 mini, for $900: no-gross trades, heavy discounts, one-price used units.

Add those three buckets, $1,050 plus $875 plus $900, and the month pays $2,825 in front-end commission. Divide by twelve cars and your true average is about $235 per car, not the $400 somebody quoted you. Half your deals were minis, and minis are an anchor. This is exactly why the average "lies": it's technically correct and practically useless, because half your volume paid a flat floor and the two big deals did most of the lifting.

Minis aren't a bug, they're a structural part of every percentage pay plan, and I only touch them at the surface here. If you want the full breakdown of why they happen and how to keep your count down, read what a mini deal really is.

New vs. used, and why used often pays better

People assume new cars pay more because the stickers are bigger. Usually it's the opposite. New-car front-end gross is thin and transparent. Invoice is a Google search away and competition compresses it, so a lot of new deals land at or near the mini once pack comes out.

Used is where the gross lives. A used unit the store bought right can carry $2,000-$3,000 of front-end gross, and even with a heavier pack (used packs sometimes run higher) the commissionable number is bigger. Take a $2,500 used deal with a $700 pack: $1,800 times 25% = $450, versus the same-gross new car that gets ground down to a mini. The reconditioning risk is real, but per-car pay potential is usually higher on the used side.

This is why two salespeople at the same store on the same rate can have completely different per-car averages. The new-car specialist grinding volume at mini-heavy grosses and the used hand turning fat pre-owned deals are playing different games, even on identical pay plans.

Volume, back-end, and spiffs reshape the number

The per-car figure isn't fixed. Three levers move it. First, volume bonuses: hit a unit tier (a bonus at 12, 15, or 20 cars is common) and that lump sum spreads back across every car you sold, quietly lifting your effective per-car pay even when the individual deals didn't change. Volume is also how you out-earn a mini-heavy mix, since more minis plus a bonus can still beat fewer minis without one.

Second, back-end. Some pay plans pay you a cut (usually smaller) of F&I gross: financing reserve, extended warranties, GAP. Many pay none at all. If yours does, a deal that was a front-end mini can still put real money in your pocket because the customer financed and bought a service contract. When financing comes up, knowing roughly what the customer's payment looks like helps you hold gross instead of caving on price; a quick auto loan calculator keeps you honest at the desk.

Third, spiffs, small immediate cash on specific units or products. A $100 spiff on an aged unit turns a $150 mini into $250 for that car. None of these show up when someone quotes you a flat "average per car," but they're often the difference between a decent month and a great one. The only average car salesman commission per car that matters is yours

Every number in this post is a common industry range, not your paycheck. Your rate might be 20% or 30%. Your pack might be $400 or $900. Your store might pay back-end or not, mini at $100 or $200, bonus at 10 cars or 18. The pay plan taped to your desk is the only document that governs what you actually make, not a stat you read online, including this one.

So do this. Take your last full month, list every deal's front-end gross, run each one through your real pack and rate with your mini floor, and average the payouts. That number, your number, is your true average commission per car. FAQ

So what's the average commission per car, in one number?
If you're forced to pick one, most salespeople on a percentage plan land somewhere in the low-to-mid hundreds per car once you blend strong deals with minis. But that blended figure describes almost none of your actual deals. A strong front-end deal can pay $500-$700, an average one $250-$350, and a mini just the $100-$200 floor. Your real average depends entirely on how many minis are in your mix, which is why the single number is misleading.
Is commission based on the price of the car or the profit?
The profit, almost always. Front-end commission is typically a percentage (commonly 20-30%) of the car's front-end gross, the selling price minus the dealership's total cost in the vehicle, after a fixed pack is subtracted, not a percentage of the sticker or sale price. On a $35,000 sale with $2,000 of gross, your commission is figured on that $2,000 (minus pack), not on the $35,000.
Why did I only make $150 on a car I sold for full price?
Because full price doesn't mean full gross. If the store had a lot of money in that unit, or a big pack came off first, the commissionable gross can be small enough that your percentage lands below the mini, so you get bumped up to the minimum, commonly $100-$200. Full-price sale, thin gross, mini payout. It happens constantly on new cars.
Do new or used cars pay more per car?
Used usually pays more per car because used front-end gross tends to be larger and less transparent than new, even though used packs can run higher. New-car gross is thin and easy for customers to research, so more new deals land at or near the mini. A used unit carrying $2,000-$3,000 of gross will out-pay most new deals on the same rate.
How do I raise my average commission per car?
Two levers move it most: hold more gross on the deals you already work (so fewer of them fall to the mini), and stack the extras (back-end if your plan pays it, spiffs on specific units, and volume bonuses that spread back across every car). Cutting your mini count is usually the fastest win. Run your real grosses through the commission calculator to see where your break-even between percentage and mini actually sits.
