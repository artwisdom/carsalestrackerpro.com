"I Want to Think About It" — What It Means and What to Say

Every rep on the floor has heard it at the desk, keys in hand, three feet from a signature. It is the most common stall in car sales and the most misread. The customer is rarely asking for time to think. They are telling you something they would rather not say out loud.

Published by Michael DubeCreator of CarSales Tracker Pro · methodology & corrections
The short version

"I want to think about it" is a polite lid on a specific concern — usually payment, trade value, a missing decision-maker, or a comparison they haven't finished. Agree first, isolate the real objection with one question, answer that one thing, and re-ask for the business. If they still leave, the deal moves to your follow-up system, not your memory.

It is a lid, not a decision

Nobody who is genuinely undecided about a $32,000 purchase spends two hours on a lot, takes a demo, and sits through a write-up. If they got that far, they want the car. What stopped them is a single unresolved item, and "let me think about it" is the socially safe way to end the conversation without arguing with you about it.

That reframe matters, because it changes your job. You are not trying to talk someone into a car they don't want. You are trying to find out what the lid is covering. Reps who treat the stall as a "no" walk the customer to the door. Reps who treat it as an unasked question get a second at-bat.

It also changes your tone. Pressure at this moment confirms every fear the customer walked in with, and a green pea leaning harder is the fastest way to turn a soft stall into a hard one.

Agree, then isolate

The move that works is boring and repeatable: take the pressure off, then ask one question that narrows the field.

"Absolutely — this is a big decision and I'd never want you to rush it. Just so I know I did my job: is it the car itself you're unsure about, or the numbers?"

That single fork does most of the work. If it's the car, you may have shown the wrong unit and you still have inventory. If it's the numbers — and it usually is — you now have something concrete to solve instead of a fog to argue with.

Two things make or break this. First, actually pause after you ask. The silence is where the real answer comes from. Second, take the answer at face value and go solve it rather than pivoting into a second close. If you handle the objection and it moves to a different one, that's normal — you're peeling, not failing.

Five stalls hiding behind the same sentence

In practice, the think-about-it stall almost always resolves into one of five things. Recognizing which one you're facing is most of the skill.

"I want to think about it."The payment is higher than the number in their head."Is the payment where you need it, or is it the total?" Then work term, down, or a different unit.
"I need to sleep on it."They think their trade is worth more than you offered.Re-walk the appraisal out loud — miles, tires, recon, market. Show the math, don't defend the number.

Payment is the one that comes up most, and it's also the one reps fumble most often by guessing at figures. Run the real numbers in front of the customer — a free auto loan calculator takes ten seconds and beats a payment you invented and have to walk back at the desk.

What it costs you to let one walk

Objection handling can feel like arguing for sport until you attach a number to it. Say your store pays a common front-end rate — our calculator defaults to 25% of gross — with a mini of $150 when a deal comes in under about $1,000 of gross.

A held deal with $2,400 of front gross pays you roughly $600. The same customer, returned two weeks later after shopping three stores, is a price fight that lands at $900 of gross and pays $225. The customer who never comes back pays $0. Across a month, saving two of those is often the gap between a mini-heavy check and a good one — and the difference between hitting a volume bonus tier and missing it by one unit.

Those are illustrative figures, not a promise; your rate, pack and mini rules decide the real spread. If you want the version with your own pay plan in it, the car salesman commission calculator will do the arithmetic, and what counts as a mini explains why thin deals pay so little.

The ones who still leave

Some will walk no matter how clean your process was, and that's the job. What separates reps who sell 18 from reps who sell 9 isn't a magic rebuttal — it's what happens after the up drives off.

Before they leave, earn the next contact: "I'll put everything in writing and text it to you tonight — what's the best number?" Then log the deal the same day with the actual objection you heard, not a vague "thinking about it." A note that reads "wants $310/mo, trade payoff $4,200 over" tells future-you exactly how to reopen the conversation. A note that reads "call back" tells you nothing.

From there it's cadence, not luck — same-day recap, a value-add on day two or three, then spaced touches. FAQ

How do you respond to "I want to think about it" in car sales?
Agree with them first so the pressure drops, then isolate with one question: is it the car, or the numbers? Solve the specific thing they name, then ask for the business again. Arguing with the stall itself almost always ends the conversation.
Is "I want to think about it" a real objection?
Rarely. It's usually a stand-in for payment, trade value, a decision-maker who isn't present, or an unfinished comparison. Treat it as a signal to ask one more question, not as a final answer.
How many times should you handle an objection before letting the customer go?
Two or three genuine attempts is a reasonable ceiling. Past that you're trading a possible sale today for a customer who won't take your call next week. End it warm, get the contact info, and put the deal in your follow-up cadence.
Should I bring in a manager (T.O.) on a think-about-it?
Often yes — a fresh face changes the dynamic and a manager can move numbers you can't. Set it up honestly ("let me get my manager, he may have room I don't") rather than springing it as a pressure tactic.
